Anna Wagner Keichline designed the original clubhouse at what is now the
American Legion Country Club. That commission connects Mount Union to a woman
of national importance in architecture, industrial design, invention, public
service, and the campaign for women's voting rights.

Born in Bellefonte on May 24, 1889, Keichline showed an early ability to turn
ideas into useful objects. At fourteen, she won first prize at the Centre
County Fair for an oak table and a walnut chest she had designed and built in
her own workshop. She studied mechanical engineering at Pennsylvania State
College during 1906–07 and then transferred to Cornell University. In 1911,
she became the fifth woman to earn an architecture degree from Cornell.

When Pennsylvania began formally registering architects, Keichline passed the
state examination. In 1920, she became the **first registered woman architect
in Pennsylvania** and one of the earliest professionally registered women
architects in the United States.

## An Architect of Everyday Life

Keichline established her practice in Bellefonte and designed more than twenty
known residential and commercial buildings in Pennsylvania, Ohio, and
Washington, D.C. Her work included homes, a church, a school, a theater,
automobile-related buildings, and community gathering places. The projects
were varied, but a common thread ran through them: carefully organized spaces
intended to work well for the people using them.

Among her recognized major works are the Bald Eagle and Nittany Valley
Presbyterian Church in Mill Hall, the Cadillac Garage and Apartments and Plaza
Theatre in Bellefonte, and the **Juniata Valley Colony Clubhouse in Mount
Union**. National architectural scholarship now lists the clubhouse alongside
these other surviving examples of her career.

## Seven Patents and the K-Brick

Keichline held six utility patents and one design patent. Her inventions
addressed the organization of domestic and building space: a collapsible sink
and washtub, an efficient kitchen arrangement, a folding apartment bed, a
portable partition, a heating and ventilation system, a toy, and a new kind
of building block.

Her best-known invention was the 1927 **K-Brick**. The hollow building unit
could be made from clay, concrete, or another suitable material. Its slots and
notches allowed it to be broken predictably into shapes needed at corners and
other construction details. The block was promoted as lightweight,
inexpensive, fire resistant, insulating, and sound resistant—a forerunner of
modern hollow masonry units. The American Ceramic Society honored Keichline's
work in 1931.

## Suffrage and Military Intelligence

Keichline's public work extended beyond architecture. She was active in the
campaign for women's suffrage and led Bellefonte's March of the Vote on July
4, 1913. During World War I, she volunteered for government service and wrote
that she wanted work that was “more difficult” and “more dangerous” than a
routine office assignment. Fluent in German, she was assigned as a special
agent in Military Intelligence in Washington, D.C., and received commendations
for her service.

These chapters are essential to understanding her. Keichline did not simply
enter a profession that rarely admitted women; she repeatedly claimed a place
in public, technical, and civic life.

## Designing the Colony Clubhouse

Contemporary reporting identifies Keichline as architect of the Colony Club
clubhouse, Charles H. Baldwin as builder, and Ralph Ganoe as construction
superintendent. Work was underway by August 1926. The building used native
stone and stucco, and newspaper accounts describe stone reclaimed from older
farm structures and a canal-era bridge.

The clubhouse was designed as the social center of a much larger recreation
complex. A broad porch opened to the main club room through multiple pairs of
French doors, connecting the interior gathering space with the grounds and
pool. The building formally opened with a dance on August 12, 1927, when the
first nine golf holes were already playable.

The surviving photographs allow the building to be read as architecture, not
just as a name in a newspaper. They show a substantial stone base anchoring
the clubhouse to the hillside, a long upper porch oriented toward outdoor
recreation, and an expansive lounge organized around a prominent stone
fireplace. The arrangement supported dances, dinners, meetings, and informal
club life while keeping the surrounding landscape and recreation grounds in
view.

## Preserving Keichline's Record

Keichline died on February 5, 1943, at the age of fifty-three. Her career was
not forgotten, but much of its documentation remained dispersed for decades.
Her grandniece, designer and historian Nancy J. Perkins, carried out extensive
research, preserved family photographs, and helped place Keichline's work in
the broader history of women in architecture and industrial design. The Anna
Keichline Papers, covering approximately 1900–1940, are now preserved by
Virginia Tech's Special Collections and University Archives.

The clubhouse gives ALCC a special responsibility within that history. Every
round, gathering, and celebration held there continues the life of a building
designed by Pennsylvania's first registered woman architect. It is not merely
a surviving clubhouse; it is a working Keichline building.
