Roy Paul Miller Davis brought together industry, banking, invention, and
community promotion in a career that stretched far beyond the golf course.
His importance to ALCC begins with the organizing meeting he helped convene
at Mount Union High School on August 26, 1924, but the experience that led him
there had been built over decades.

Archival Princeton records place Davis with the Class of 1898 and state that
he studied there for three years. A later Princeton alumni memorial described
him as a manufacturer, banker, mineral expert, real-estate investor, and
inventor. It listed leadership positions with United States Refractories
Corporation, Refractories Machine Manufacturing Corporation, Kistler Real
Estate and Improvement Company, and the Central National Bank of Mount Union.
Those overlapping interests help explain why Davis imagined the Colony Club
as both a recreational institution and a regional development project.

## Industry and Invention

Davis's strongest documented inventions grew directly from Mount Union's
refractory industry. In 1924, the United States Patent Office granted him a
patent for an automatic brickmaking machine designed to increase production
while reducing strain on molds and their supporting equipment. A second 1924
patent covered a related method and apparatus for forming brick. These were
not hobby inventions; they addressed the machinery at the center of the local
industrial economy.

Davis also served as president of the Central National Bank of Mount Union.
A surviving Series of 1929 twenty-dollar national-bank note bears his
signature as president, preserving a direct physical connection to that part
of his career.

## Service During the 1918 Influenza Epidemic

Davis's community role became especially visible during the influenza
epidemic of 1918. Contemporary reporting described approximately 100 cases
among Kistler's population of about 800. The school was converted into an
emergency hospital and reached 21 patients within its 24-bed capacity. The
report identified Davis among the leaders of the relief effort as nurses and
other workers cared for the sick under extremely difficult conditions.

This episode adds an important dimension to his record. Six years before the
Colony Club organizing meeting, Davis was already using his position and
industrial resources to respond to a community emergency.

## An Ambitious Club and Summer Community

Three days after the August 1924 organizing meeting, the *Mount Union Times*
reported 128 stockholders and charter members. The proposal joined a country
club to a summer-cottage development along the Juniata River. Golf was central,
but Davis's plan also included cottage lots, rental homes, roads, water and
sewer service, a clubhouse, swimming, tennis, boating, fishing, and
trapshooting.

By August 1926, newspaper reporting described 585 acres assembled from three
farms. Davis's own prospectus promoted roughly 600 acres and explained how
land sales, rentals, visitors, dues, and recreation were expected to support
the development. Those figures document the scale of his vision; they do not
prove that every proposed cottage or lot was completed.

## A Complicated Financial Record

Davis's central role also places him at the heart of the project's financial
story. In a December 1929 public letter, he distinguished the Juniata Valley
Realty Corporation, which owned and developed land, from the Colony Club,
which operated the recreational institution. He discussed borrowing, credit
pressure, and threats of bankruptcy while defending both organizations. The
letter is valuable evidence of his position, but it was not an independent
financial audit.

In 1930, stockholders considered increasing authorized indebtedness while the
development continued planning additional cottages. The surviving record
therefore shows both achievement and exposure: Davis helped create an
extraordinary regional recreation community, but its business structure was
under strain well before the destructive flood of 1936.

## Sta-Neet and a Later California Career

Later in life, Davis became closely associated with the Sta-Neet Corporation
and its adjustable razor-and-comb hair trimmer. Contemporary publicity
credited the product with more than one million sales and described Davis as
the driving businessman behind its rapid commercial growth. A Princeton
alumni memorial also credited his California operation with employing workers
with disabilities, including blind assembly workers.

The invention credit requires precision. The principal 1949 Sta-Neet patent
names **Sanford E. A. Sanders** as inventor and Sta-Neet Corporation as
assignee—not Davis. Davis did, however, hold or acquire rights in related
hair-trimmer designs, including a later patent that references his own pending
application. He should therefore be remembered as Sta-Neet's president,
promoter, and an active participant in its product development, rather than as
the sole inventor of the original patented comb.

## Blue Springs and Davis's Legacy

Davis and his family also maintained a Juniata River estate known as **Blue
Springs**. Newspaper accounts connect the property with gardens, family
weddings, and river recreation. Local recollections describe a bridge linking
the estate side of the river with the Colony Club grounds. The former Davis
property later became part of the land associated with Beacon Lodge, the camp
developed to serve blind and visually impaired people.

Davis's legacy is consequently larger—and more complicated—than a single
founding meeting. He helped modernize an important local industry, led a Mount
Union bank, responded during a public-health crisis, pursued inventions and
businesses across the country, and promoted the riverside development from
which today's American Legion Country Club emerged. His ambitions sometimes
outran the enterprise's finances, but the institution he helped set in motion
survived them.
